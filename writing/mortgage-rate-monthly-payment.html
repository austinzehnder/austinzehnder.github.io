What a mortgage rate actually does to a monthly payment

By Austin Zehnder, San Francisco finance and real estate professional, founder of Zehndra.

Most buyers shop the price of a house. The rate, quietly, shops them back. On a 30-year fixed loan, principal and interest are calculated as: payment = loan × [r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n − 1], where r is the monthly rate and n is 360 payments.

Worked example, principal and interest only: borrow $800,000 for 30 years. At 3%, the payment is about $3,373 a month. At 6%, it is about $4,797. At 7%, about $5,322. The house did not change. The loan amount did not change. A four-point rate move added roughly $1,950 a month — about $23,400 a year.

That is why the same house can feel affordable in one rate regime and impossible in another, and why a small rate improvement is worth real money: on that $800,000 loan, dropping from 7% to 6% saves about $525 a month, or $6,300 a year, without renegotiating the price at all.

Three rules Austin Zehnder uses

  1. Always compare principal and interest first. Taxes, insurance, and HOA dues matter, but they do not move with the rate. Isolate the rate effect before you add the rest.
  2. Translate the rate into dollars per month, not just percent. "Rates are up 1%" sounds abstract. "$525 more a month" is a decision.
  3. Do not confuse a lower rate with a good deal, or a higher rate with a bad one. Price, loan amount, how long you will hold, and whether you will refinance all matter. The rate is one input, just a very large one.

For a real-world version of this, see the @housingcharted payment duel: on a $1 million home with 20% down, principal and interest was about $3,224 a month at 2.65% versus about $5,539 a month at 7.40% — a $2,315 monthly gap on the same house.
